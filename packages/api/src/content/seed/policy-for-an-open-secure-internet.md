Technology policy is often written in response to a crisis: a major breach, a wave of fraud, a new platform nobody anticipated. Rules made in a hurry tend to be either too broad, restricting legitimate use, or too narrow, missing the next problem entirely.

## Security and openness are not opposites

An open internet depends on trust. People share information, bank and run businesses online only when they believe systems are reasonably safe. Strong security, in turn, benefits from openness: shared threat intelligence, open standards and transparent incident reporting make everyone more resilient.

## Bring everyone to the table

The multistakeholder approach brings government, industry, academia, the technical community and civil society into the same conversation. Each sees risks the others miss:

- Regulators understand legal and public-interest obligations
- Operators know what is technically feasible and what it costs
- Researchers and civil society highlight effects on privacy and rights

## Principles over prescriptions

Rules that name specific technologies age quickly. Policy that sets outcomes, such as timely breach notification, minimum security baselines for critical services and clear accountability, stays relevant as tools change.

## Build local capacity

Policy only works if people can implement it. Investment in incident response teams, training and awareness turns regulations on paper into real protection.

## Review and adapt

Technology moves faster than legislation. Built-in review cycles, informed by incident data and stakeholder feedback, keep policy aligned with reality.

The goal is not to choose between an open internet and a secure one. It is to design governance where each strengthens the other.
