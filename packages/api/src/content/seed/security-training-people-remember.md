Most people who click a phishing link have already sat through security training. The problem is rarely a lack of information; it is that the information was delivered in a way that did not stick.

## Make it about their work

Generic examples feel distant. A finance team responds far better to a fake invoice that looks like one they process every week than to an abstract lesson about "social engineering". Tailor scenarios to each department's real workflows.

## Short and frequent beats long and annual

A three-hour session once a year is forgotten within weeks. Fifteen-minute sessions every month, each focused on a single habit, build lasting behaviour:

- Checking the sender's real address before acting on a request
- Verifying payment changes through a second channel
- Locking screens and reporting lost devices immediately

## Reward reporting, not just avoiding mistakes

If staff fear blame, they hide mistakes. Celebrate the person who reports a suspicious email, even if they clicked it first. Fast reporting turns a potential breach into a contained incident.

## Practise, then measure

Simulated phishing exercises show whether training works. Track report rates as well as click rates; a rising report rate is one of the best signs that awareness is improving.

## Leaders go first

When managers attend the same sessions and follow the same rules, security becomes part of the culture instead of an IT requirement. Training that starts at the top is taken seriously everywhere else.

Good training is less about teaching people everything and more about building a few reliable instincts. Those instincts are what protect an organization on an ordinary, busy day.
