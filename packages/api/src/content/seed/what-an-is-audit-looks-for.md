Many organizations treat an information systems audit as an annual hurdle: gather some documents, answer some questions, fix whatever gets flagged. That approach passes audits but rarely improves security. A good audit is a structured look at whether your controls actually do what you believe they do.

## Governance before technology

The first questions are rarely technical. Auditors want to know who owns information security, how decisions are made, and whether policies are current and approved. A firewall configured perfectly means little if nobody is accountable for reviewing its rules.

Expect to be asked for:

- An approved information security policy and the date it was last reviewed
- A named owner for each critical system
- Evidence that risks are assessed and tracked, not just listed once

## Access control is where most findings live

Excess privileges, shared accounts and forgotten user IDs of staff who left months ago are among the most common findings. Auditors will sample user lists and compare them against HR records.

A simple quarterly access review, signed off by system owners, prevents most of these issues.

## Change and patch management

Unplanned changes cause outages and open security gaps. Auditors look for a documented change process: requests, approvals, testing and rollback plans. For patching, they want to see a defined window for critical updates and evidence that it is followed.

## Backups you have actually restored

A backup that has never been restored is an assumption, not a control. Keep records of periodic restore tests, including how long recovery took compared with your stated recovery objectives.

## Logging and incident response

Can you tell who did what, and when? Centralized logs with sensible retention, plus an incident response plan that people have rehearsed, show that you can detect and respond rather than just prevent.

## Preparing without the scramble

The best preparation is continuous: keep evidence as part of normal operations rather than collecting it the week before the audit. Treat each finding as an input to your risk register, assign an owner and a date, and close the loop.

An audit done this way stops being a hurdle and becomes one of the most useful health checks your organization gets each year.
